At 12:33 AM I realized I had about 60 people coming to an AI club lunch at 11:30 and no food. So I typed one sentence to Claude: pizza for 60 from the Papa John's near campus, delivered to Huntsman.

By the next afternoon that sentence had turned into three separate jobs, run by three agents, and my part was a handful of keystrokes.

## What happened

The first agent drove my browser like a person would. It found the store, built the cart, and when I said 20 pizzas felt like a lot, it rebuilt the order as 16 around a bundle deal I'd never have looked for at midnight. That came out about $137 under menu price. I typed my card in and went to sleep.

The second agent ran on a schedule. In October I'd accidentally started a paid trial on BILL, the payments platform my club uses, while trying to join the club's account. It was charging me $52.92. At 9 AM the agent opened BILL's support chat, explained what happened to their bot, asked for a human, and got the charge waived before my first class.

The third agent did the paperwork. It read the club's reimbursement instructions, pulled the receipt from my email, filled the expense form, and checked it against the one a teammate had filed for me last month. Then it hit a wall. BILL wanted a Social Security number to pay me, and as a Canadian student I don't have one. The agent told me there was no legitimate way around it.

I didn't believe that. A third of my class is international, so somebody had solved this already. I told it to keep looking, and it found the answer in a slide deck sitting in the club's finance drive: students without an SSN use the student association's tax ID. Not long after, I was set up to get paid.

Four things stuck with me.

## 1. The hard part was never the thinking

None of these tasks needed intelligence. Ordering pizza, disputing a charge and filing an expense are things any of us can do. What they need is time and attention: logging in, finding the right page, reading the manual, waiting in a queue, remembering to follow up.

That's what agents remove. And when the cost of doing a chore drops close to zero, you stop asking "is this worth my time?" I'd been ignoring that $52.92 for a month because fighting it felt like an hour of my life. It took the agent a few minutes at 9 AM while I was getting ready for class.

## 2. When both sides have agents, friction stops working

A lot of businesses quietly make money from friction. Subscriptions people forget to cancel. Refunds that need three emails. Claims that get denied the first time because most people don't appeal.

All of that assumes the customer gets tired. An agent doesn't. Mine talked to BILL's bot, then to a person, and it would have kept going. Soon there won't be a person on either side, only my agent following my rules and their agent following theirs. The outcome then depends on whose rules were written better, and what each side is allowed to concede.

I think that pushes companies one of two ways. Either they add more friction aimed at bots (phone-only cancellation, identity checks), or they accept that people will cancel, dispute and claim what they're owed, and price for it. My guess is the second one wins, because the first one also annoys every real customer.

## 3. Agents give up too early, and confidently

This was the part I didn't expect. The agent didn't make anything up. It did the opposite. It saw a rule ("we need an SSN"), concluded the rule was final, and politely handed the problem back to me with an email draft to the finance team.

One piece of base-rate reasoning got it unstuck: hundreds of international students get reimbursed through this system every year, so the answer has to exist somewhere. Once I said that, it went looking in the right place and found it in a few minutes.

I think this is the real skill with agents right now, and it's closer to managing a sharp new analyst than to writing prompts. They're fast and thorough, and they'll treat the first wall they hit as the end. Your job is knowing which walls are real. The question that worked for me was simple: who else has had this problem, and what did they do?

## 4. The human's job is moving from doing to setting limits

This time I typed my card number and my bank details myself. I don't think that lasts. Payment companies are already building ways for agents to spend money, and the obvious next step is giving an agent a card with a limit, an allowlist of merchants, and a rule like "never agree to a recurring charge."

So the gate moves. Today the human types the sensitive thing. Soon the human sets the policy once (how much, with whom, what it can never agree to) and steps in only at the edges. That's a different job. It looks a lot more like being a CFO for your own life than doing your own admin.

## Where this matters most

For an individual, this is the admin tax that eats a few hours a week: reimbursements, cancellations, insurance, forms, hold music. None of it is hard, and all of it is easy to put off.

For a small business owner it's bigger, because there's no back office. The owner is the accounts payable team, the procurement team, and the person reading a 40-page grant manual to find the one line that says they qualify. That last one is pretty much what happened to me. The answer existed. It was buried in a document nobody reads. A big company pays people to find those lines. A three-person shop now can too, for the cost of a subscription.

The pizza was a small thing. What I keep thinking about is the shape of it. One sentence from me, three agents doing the work, and my real contribution was two things I wouldn't hand over yet and one moment of saying "no, keep going."
