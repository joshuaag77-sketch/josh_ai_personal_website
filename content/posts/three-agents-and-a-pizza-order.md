At 12:33 AM I realized I had about 60 people coming to an AI club lunch at 11:30 and no food. The kati roll place I wanted didn't open until 11.

So I typed one sentence to Claude: pizza for 60 from the Papa John's near campus, delivered to Huntsman, 11:30. By the time I had to do anything with my own hands, a small team of agents had split up the night's errands.

## What actually happened

The ordering agent drove my browser like a person would. It opened Papa John's, confirmed the store and the address, and built a cart. I said 20 pizzas felt like a lot, so it rebuilt the order as 16 and found a bundle deal I'd never have looked for at midnight, about $137 under menu price. I typed my card and the tip myself and went to sleep.

The second agent ran on a schedule. Back in October I'd accidentally started a paid BILL trial trying to join my club's reimbursement account, and it was charging me $52.92. At 9 AM an agent opened BILL's support chat, explained what happened, asked for a human and got the balance waived before my 10:15 class.

The third agent did the paperwork. It read the club's reimbursement instructions, pulled the receipt out of my email, filled the expense form and merged it into the one PDF the treasurer asks for. I asked it to check its work against the reimbursement a teammate had filed for me in October, and it caught three things that didn't line up.

Then it hit a wall. BILL wanted an SSN to pay me. I'm Canadian on an F-1, so I don't have one. Claude told me there was no legit way around it and drafted an email asking the finance team for help.

I didn't buy it. A third of my class is international, there had to be a way. I told it to keep digging, and it found the answer in a slide deck in our finance drive: students without an SSN use the student association's EIN. Done.

## Agents talking to agents

The BILL chat is the part I keep thinking about. This time it was my agent on one side and their support bot on the other, until a person stepped in. Soon there won't be a person on either side.

A lot of subscription revenue comes from people who meant to cancel and never got around to it. The friction is the business model. If everyone has something that follows up at 9 AM, politely, every time, that friction stops working. Companies will either add more of it (phone-only cancellation, bot checks) or price as if people actually cancel.

Negotiation changes too. Their bot follows a policy about what it can concede. Mine follows a policy about what it can agree to, and mine can't agree to pay anything. When both sides are policies, the outcome is mostly decided by whoever wrote better rules ahead of time.

## Where the value is

Pizza is a small example, but the pattern is everywhere. It's the boring admin between you and the thing you actually wanted.

For an individual, that's reimbursements, cancellations, insurance claims, the form you've been ignoring for three weeks, the 40 minutes on hold. None of it is hard. It just takes time and attention, and an agent has plenty of both.

For a small business owner it's bigger. Chasing a vendor refund, reordering supplies, reading a 30-page grant or tax credit manual to find the one line that says you qualify. That last one is basically what happened here. The answer existed. It was buried in a document nobody reads.

## What still needs a person

Two things stayed with me. I typed the card and the bank details, because an agent shouldn't hold those. And I was the one who said keep going when the agent decided a problem was unsolvable.

The agent did most of the work, and did it better than I would have at 1 AM. But it stopped at a wall that turned out to be a door. For now I think the real skill with these tools is closer to managing than prompting: knowing when to trust the first answer and when to push back on it.
