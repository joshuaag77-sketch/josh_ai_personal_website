I just finished a week on teams and leadership at Wharton. I went in expecting soft-skills filler. I came out with a stack of studies, and most of them say the same thing: the leader matters less than the conditions the leader sets before anyone shows up. Here are the eight that stuck, roughly in the order they landed.

## The leader matters less than the setup

One study separated two things: how a team was designed, and how well the leader coached it day to day. Design predicted performance about forty times better than coaching did.

Good coaching helped teams that were already well designed. It did almost nothing for badly designed ones, and bad coaching, meaning diagnosing the problem and telling people what to fix, made struggling teams worse. Hackman calls our instinct to credit whoever is standing out front the leader attribution error.

## Half the room is holding back

Managers were asked how often they had privately held back their real input on a team. They said it happened on 56% of their teams.

The most common reason, at 73%, was not fear of the boss. It was that someone with more expertise was in the room. The second was that somebody had already made a decent argument. Both feel like good judgment while you are doing them, which is why nobody notices.

## Brainstorming out loud makes the group worse

This one has forty years of replication behind it. Groups generating ideas together produce fewer and worse ideas than the same people working alone. Only one person can talk at a time, everyone is managing how they come across, and people drift toward whatever has already been said.

Longer meetings and bigger rooms have both been tested and neither helps. What works is having people generate alone first, then combine and rank together.

## You are arguing about conclusions, not data

Argyris called it the ladder of inference. You observe something, select a few details, add meaning, form an assumption, reach a conclusion, act. The whole climb takes about a second, and only the bottom rung and the top one are visible to anyone else. So two people trade conclusions and neither has seen a single step of the other's reasoning.

The fix is to say what you saw that got you there, then ask what they saw.

## Some conflict helps

A meta-analysis across 116 studies found that task conflict, arguing about the work itself, can improve performance. Relationship conflict never does.

Process conflict is the one to watch. That is disagreement about roles, ownership, and how things get run. If nobody says "we disagree about the process," the only explanation left for the friction is each other, and it turns personal.

## Your mood is part of the culture

Every organization has an emotional culture, including the ones where the culture is suppression. It travels through faces and tone more than words, so you are broadcasting it whether you mean to or not.

One executive in the research was so reliably angry that his staff had text message codes for the stages of it, red for the face, vein for the forehead. That is a culture, and he built it without meaning to.

## Charisma can be taught

Managers were randomly assigned to a short course on inspirational leadership behaviours. Three months later their bosses, peers and direct reports rated them 22% more charismatic. Personality barely predicted any of it. Transformational leadership turns out to be a set of behaviours you can learn, which I found encouraging and a little deflating at the same time.

## Norms beat rules

Chatman's version is the one I keep coming back to. Rules only cover situations you can predict, so they cannot produce anything outstanding, because outstanding is defined by the situation nobody anticipated. Norms can.

A strong culture needs two things. Agreement about what matters, and intensity about it. Plenty of places have the first. Everyone can recite the values and nobody will stay late for them.

## What I take from it

Every one of these points the same way. We overweight the person at the front and underweight the conditions they set before anyone showed up. The variables with the biggest effect sizes are the ones you decide in advance: how the team is designed, how people generate ideas, whether someone names a process disagreement early.

I got a live version of this at the pre-term off-site in Delaware. One exercise was lifting a bucket with ropes, no touching allowed. Our first instinct was to use everyone and every rope. A two-person version beat it easily, because fewer hands meant less torque fighting itself. I had not read Hackman yet. The exercise made his point before I got to the reading.
